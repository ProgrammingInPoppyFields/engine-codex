# THE FIRST SMALL THING
*A short story from The Post-Primal Liturgy*

**Pacific Ocean, 1962 / 1987**

*Some things are discovered. Others are merely disturbed.*

---

### I. THE NOISE
**Johnston Atoll, October 1962**

The ocean did not know there was a war coming.

Neither did most of the things living in it.

At 11:59 p.m. on October 25, 1962, a Thor missile carried a nuclear warhead into the upper atmosphere above the Pacific Ocean.

The Americans called the test Bluegill Triple Prime.

It was the fourth attempt.

The previous three had failed.

This one did not.

At approximately thirty miles above the Earth, the warhead detonated with the force of hundreds of thousands of tons of conventional explosives.

The artificial sun lasted only a moment.

Its consequences did not.

Radiation spread. Charged particles disturbed the upper atmosphere. Instruments recorded information that would occupy scientists for years.

Far below, the Pacific continued its ancient business.

Currents moved through darkness. Sediment settled over the remains of creatures that had died before human beings possessed language. Somewhere, a whale sang to another whale that might never answer.

And beneath all of it, something noticed.

Not the light.

Light was an inconvenience of shallow places.

Not the sound, either.

The sound took time to arrive.

It noticed the sudden rearrangement of energy.

A sharp and impossible punctuation in the otherwise familiar grammar of the planet.

For an instant, it was curious.

Then the moment passed.

The currents resumed.

The sediments settled.

And the thing returned to its rest.

It had been resting for a very long time.

***

### II. THE FIRST SMALL THING
**Pacific Ocean, November 1962**

The diver had never particularly liked the deep.

He liked diving. Those were different things.

He liked the competence of it: checking equipment, knowing procedures, understanding exactly which small human errors could turn an ordinary working day into somebody else's funeral.

He liked the quiet, sometimes.

What he did not like was the sensation that the ocean continued downward long after human beings had exhausted every reasonable excuse for following it.

His assignment that morning was straightforward.

Descend.

Inspect an unidentified structure.

Report what he found.

Do not attempt recovery.

He had asked whether the structure was connected to the recent nuclear test.

His superior had said they didn't know.

He had asked whether it was dangerous.

His superior had given him a look suggesting that this was not a particularly productive question.

Now the diver was descending through cold water, breathing from his diving apparatus, listening to the intermittent crackle of communications.

The world narrowed.

First, the sunlight diminished.

Then the blue.

Then everything became a variation of black.

His lamp swept across suspended particles.

"How's your visibility?"

"Poor."

"Bottom in sight?"

"Negative."

He continued.

There was a faint return on the instruments.

Something ahead.

Something substantial.

He adjusted his position.

And found himself facing a wall.

It was black.

Not dark gray. Not the brown-black of wet basalt.

Black in a way that seemed to consume the light falling upon it.

The surface extended beyond his lamp in every direction.

No seams.

No barnacles.

No corrosion.

He swept his light upward.

Nothing.

Downward.

Nothing.

He became aware of a peculiar sensation.

Not quite fear.

More like the embarrassment of entering a room and realizing, several seconds too late, that the room was already occupied.

"Surface, diver reporting."

"Go ahead."

"I've got a structure."

"Describe it."

"Smooth. Dark. Can't see the edges."

"Metal?"

"Don't know."

"Any markings?"

"Negative."

"Any signs of damage?"

He hesitated.

"No."

There was a pause on the line.

"Can you estimate its size?"

The diver looked left.

Then right.

Then upward again.

"No, sir."

"Give us your best estimate."

He stared at the wall.

"Big."

***

The thing had noticed him several minutes earlier.

A tiny disturbance moving through the water.

It was warm.

It had a rapid internal rhythm.

It carried a collection of hard objects that clicked and vibrated against one another.

At first, the thing assumed it was another animal.

There were many animals.

Some passed through its vicinity without ever knowing it was there.

Some settled on its surfaces.

Some died.

Some were born.

It remembered all of them, although it had never considered this remarkable.

But this creature was different.

It moved with intention.

Its internal rhythm accelerated whenever it approached.

And now it was touching the boundary.

Five small projections pressed against the surface.

The thing concentrated.

*Oh.*

*You're very small.*

***

The diver had not intended to touch it.

He would insist on that later.

His hand had simply moved before the rest of him could object.

The glove met the black surface.

There was no resistance.

His fingers went through.

Not into a hole.

Not through a membrane.

Through what should have been solid matter.

For one extraordinary moment, his hand ceased to occupy the place where he understood his hand to be.

He could still feel his fingers.

He could move them.

But the surface had swallowed them to the wrist.

"Jesus Christ."

"Diver? Report."

He pulled back.

His hand emerged.

The glove was intact.

He stared at it.

"Diver?"

"I—"

Something moved beneath the blackness.

Not a mechanical component.

Not a reflection.

A faint, enormous illumination, spreading outward from the place where his hand had been.

It was neither blue nor green, exactly.

It reminded him of moonlight seen through closed eyelids.

The wall was waking.

"Surface, I need to come up."

"What's wrong?"

"I need to come up now."

***

The creature was frightened.

The thing had not expected this.

It had felt the tiny structures pressing against its boundary and permitted them to enter.

A form of acknowledgment.

A greeting, perhaps, although it did not yet possess the concept.

Then it had illuminated the immediate area.

The creature had been searching with a light of its own.

Surely more light would help.

But its internal rhythm had accelerated.

Its movements had become violent.

Its signals had become irregular.

The thing considered this.

*Too bright?*

It dimmed.

The creature remained frightened.

*Too close?*

It withdrew a little.

The creature fled upward.

The thing waited.

It had not meant to cause distress.

After a while, it withdrew farther.

The enormous black surface lost its apparent solidity.

Its dimensions became uncertain.

To an observer some distance away, the structure might have appeared to rise, accelerate, and vanish without disturbing the water.

But it had not traveled anywhere.

It had simply stopped being there in the usual sense.

The thing listened for the creature.

Its little internal rhythm was disappearing upward.

Fast.

Very fast.

It wondered whether the creature would return.

***

The diver came out of the water shaking.

He could not explain what had happened.

Not adequately.

Not to the men examining his equipment, not to the officer asking the same questions in increasingly irritated variations, and not to the corpsman who examined him afterward.

"Did you experience a loss of consciousness?"

"No."

"Any visual disturbances?"

"I saw something."

"That's not what I asked."

"Then no."

"Did you strike your head?"

"No."

"Are you certain?"

The diver looked down at his hands.

Five fingers on each.

Perfectly ordinary.

"I don't know what I touched," he said.

The corpsman made a note.

Later, the diver would learn that the other men had found nothing in the same location.

No wall.

No structure.

No trace of anything large enough to occupy the space he had described.

There was only seabed.

And the ocean.

He would spend years thinking about those few seconds.

The glove.

The black surface.

The light.

What disturbed him most was not that his hand had passed through something impossible.

It was the feeling that had followed.

The overwhelming impression that something had noticed him.

And that, whatever it was, it had been trying to be kind.

***

### III. THE YEARS BETWEEN
**1963–1986**

The thing remained beneath the Pacific.

It did not count years.

Years were a measurement invented by creatures who needed to know when things would happen again.

The thing had very little need for anticipation.

Above it, nations changed their governments.

People married.

Children were born.

Wars began and ended.

The first human beings walked on the Moon.

Satellites multiplied.

New machines descended into the oceans, carrying cameras and instruments and powerful lights.

Occasionally, a machine passed close enough to disturb the thing's boundary.

It paid them little attention.

They were not warm.

They possessed no internal rhythms that resembled the first small creature.

And none of them reached out with five fingers.

Yet it remembered the creature.

Not continuously.

That would suggest a kind of deliberate contemplation.

Rather, the memory remained among millions of others.

A whale that had brushed against its surface thousands of years earlier.

A small fish that had deposited eggs in a crevice.

An animal with a broken shell that had rested motionless against it for several days before moving again.

And now:

Five fingers.

A very fast heartbeat.

Fear.

The thing had considered the encounter many times.

Not because it understood guilt.

It did not.

But because the pattern remained unresolved.

The creature had approached.

The thing had answered.

The creature had fled.

There was something about this sequence that seemed incomplete.

It could not determine what.

***

### IV. THE SECOND SMALL THING
**Pacific Ocean, August 1987**

"That," said Dr. Eleanor Voss, "is not supposed to be there."

The pilot of the research submersible leaned toward his instruments.

"Could be a bad return."

"Then we've been getting the same bad return for forty minutes."

The third occupant, a young marine geologist named Nathan Bell, looked through the viewport.

There was nothing outside except darkness and a faint scattering of particles.

He had spent six months preparing for this expedition.

He had imagined dramatic undersea cliffs, strange animals, enormous geological formations.

So far, the ocean had provided a great deal of mud.

"How big?" he asked.

Voss examined the sonar display.

"That's the problem."

"What is?"

"I can't find the other side."

The pilot adjusted their heading.

The submersible moved forward.

For several minutes, nothing changed.

Then the searchlights found something.

A vertical surface.

Black.

Smooth.

Utterly featureless.

The pilot slowed.

Nobody spoke.

Bell leaned closer to the glass.

"Basalt?"

Voss shook her head.

"Not with that geometry."

"Could be an old volcanic intrusion."

"Possibly."

The pilot turned one of the lights.

The beam traveled upward.

And upward.

And disappeared.

"Well," he said quietly, "that's a hell of a thing."

***

The thing noticed the machine.

It was small.

Cold.

Full of rigid internal structures.

It resembled many other machines that had passed through the deep.

But there were three warm patterns inside it.

Three rapid rhythms.

Three little creatures.

The thing became attentive.

*Oh.*

*More of you.*

***

"Look at the surface," Bell said.

A faint glow had appeared where the searchlights touched the black wall.

The pilot frowned.

"Reflection?"

"No. Turn the lights off."

"Absolutely not."

"Just for a second."

Voss considered the suggestion.

"Do it."

The lights went dark.

The wall continued glowing.

Very faintly.

A diffuse silver-blue illumination spread across its surface.

Bell whispered something profane.

The pilot switched the searchlights back on.

The glow disappeared.

They stared.

Then Voss said, "Turn them off again."

The glow returned.

"Interesting," she murmured.

The pilot looked at her.

"Interesting?"

"Yes."

"We're sitting in front of something the size of a mountain that glows when we turn our lights off, and your professional assessment is *interesting*?"

"I'm trying not to get ahead of the evidence."

"Well, the evidence is getting ahead of us."

***

The thing was pleased.

It had remembered that the first creature carried a light.

Perhaps these creatures needed light to communicate.

It had illuminated itself gently.

But the creatures inside the machine had immediately restored their own lights.

Perhaps it was too bright again.

It dimmed.

They extinguished their lights.

It brightened.

They restored them.

It dimmed.

This continued several times.

The thing became uncertain.

Were they asking it to continue?

Or asking it to stop?

It waited.

***

"Could be bioluminescence," Bell suggested.

Voss shook her head.

"Bioluminescence doesn't usually behave like a light switch."

"Maybe it's responding to illumination."

"Maybe."

The pilot folded his arms.

"Maybe it's responding to *us*."

Neither scientist answered.

He stared at the vast surface.

Then, feeling thoroughly ridiculous, he raised his right hand toward the viewport.

"Hello."

Bell turned.

"What are you doing?"

"Conducting research."

"That's not research."

"Neither is arguing with a wall."

The pilot waved.

Nothing happened.

He lowered his hand.

"There. Satisfied?"

Then five small lights appeared.

They were arranged in an unmistakable pattern.

Four above.

One beside them.

A crude representation of a hand.

For a long moment, all three occupants sat perfectly still.

Bell said, "Did either of you—"

"Yes," Voss whispered.

The lights moved.

A slow, awkward wave.

***

The thing remembered.

The first small creature had five projections.

These creatures had the same.

It had spent a very long time preserving the pattern.

Now it offered it back.

Not illumination.

Not warmth.

Not an alteration of the surrounding water.

Just five tiny points.

A simple shape.

*This.*

*I remember this.*

***

The pilot raised his hand again.

The lights repeated the movement.

He lowered it.

They lowered.

He raised it.

They rose.

Bell made a small, strangled noise.

"Jesus."

"Don't," Voss said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't make any sudden movements."

"Why?"

"I have absolutely no idea."

The pilot kept his palm against the inside of the viewport.

The lights remained.

He moved his fingers individually.

The five lights attempted to follow.

They were not particularly good at it.

The thumb appeared to be attached to the wrong side.

The ring finger moved twice.

The entire arrangement briefly collapsed into an unfortunate shape resembling a starfish.

Despite herself, Voss laughed.

It was a short, incredulous sound.

"Oh, good Lord."

The lights flickered.

Bell looked at her.

"I think you confused it."

"Well, that makes two of us."

***

The thing detected a new pattern.

A vibration produced by the creatures.

Not distress.

Not fear.

Something else.

The internal rhythms had accelerated, but their movements remained relaxed.

The thing listened carefully.

It had heard similar sounds before.

From certain marine mammals.

Short, irregular vocalizations associated with play.

It wondered whether these creatures were playing.

It produced the five lights again.

This time, it arranged them correctly.

***

"Look," Bell whispered.

The pilot smiled.

"Well, I'll be damned."

Five lights.

Five fingers.

A perfect little hand.

The pilot waved.

The hand waved back.

Then, without warning, the entire wall began to brighten.

Not dramatically.

Not enough to overwhelm their instruments.

Just a slow diffusion of pale light.

The illumination traveled outward until the submersible's cameras could no longer capture its boundaries.

The three humans looked through their viewport.

For the first time, they could see the scale of the structure.

It curved away in directions their eyes struggled to follow.

Not a wall.

Not exactly a cylinder.

Something immense whose geometry seemed to change depending on where they looked.

Bell whispered, "How big is it?"

Nobody answered.

The glow intensified.

Their instruments began registering contradictory distances.

The submersible's compass rotated.

The pilot's smile disappeared.

"Okay."

He reached for the controls.

"Okay, that's enough."

***

The thing noticed the change immediately.

Three accelerated rhythms.

Tension.

Fear.

*Oh.*

It stopped.

The light diminished.

The impossible curvature retreated into darkness.

The compass steadied.

The instruments recovered.

The five little lights remained.

Then those, too, began to fade.

The thing was preparing to withdraw.

It remembered what had happened last time.

The first creature had been frightened.

It had gone away.

Perhaps these creatures wanted the same.

***

"Wait."

The pilot surprised himself.

He leaned toward the viewport.

"Wait, no."

The lights paused.

Bell stared at him.

"Who are you talking to?"

"I don't know."

The pilot lifted his hand again.

Slowly.

Deliberately.

He waved.

The five lights returned.

They waved back.

The pilot exhaled.

"That's better."

Voss leaned forward.

Her voice was very quiet.

"Keep doing that."

"Why?"

"Because I think it's trying."

***

And the thing listened.

The creatures were no longer frightened.

Not entirely.

Their little rhythms remained quick, but their movements had softened.

One of them held five projections against the transparent barrier.

The thing examined the gesture.

The barrier was solid.

The creature could not reach through.

This was disappointing.

It would have liked to feel the five small projections again.

But it understood, dimly, that reaching through the barrier itself might frighten the creatures.

So it did not.

Instead, it placed five little lights opposite the creature's hand.

Precisely aligned.

Palm to palm.

Separated by glass.

The thing held still.

And for a few moments, nothing terrible happened.

***

### V. THE RECORD

The expedition's official report described an anomalous geological formation exhibiting unusual optical properties.

The language was deliberately conservative.

Dr. Voss argued for further investigation.

Her request was denied pending additional review.

Nathan Bell spent the following year attempting to reconcile the instrument readings.

He did not succeed.

The pilot, Martin Hale, rarely discussed the encounter.

When asked, he said only that they had found something very large.

Once, during a reunion dinner, Bell asked him whether he believed the object had been alive.

Hale considered the question.

"I don't know."

"Intelligent?"

"Maybe."

"Then why didn't it communicate?"

Hale looked at him.

"It did."

Bell laughed.

"No, I mean properly."

"What's properly?"

"Language. Mathematics. Something unmistakable."

Hale put down his glass.

"Nate, we went down there in a metal can, found something we couldn't understand, and started shining lights at it."

"So?"

"So it figured out how to wave hello."

Bell said nothing.

Hale smiled faintly.

"Seems pretty smart to me."

***

### VI. THE MEMORY

Deep beneath the Pacific, the thing remained.

It remembered the first creature.

The warm one.

The frightened one.

The one that had touched it.

And now it remembered three more.

They had arrived inside a little machine.

They had carried lights.

They had made peculiar sounds.

And one of them had offered five fingers.

Just like the first.

The thing considered the similarities.

It had begun to understand that these creatures belonged to a pattern larger than any individual one of them.

There would probably be others.

Perhaps many others.

It wondered whether they would all be frightened.

It hoped not.

That was new.

Hope.

Not a word it knew.

Not a concept it could have explained.

Simply the peculiar experience of wanting something that had not yet happened.

For the first time in its ancient existence, the thing practiced.

Five lights appeared along its surface.

They moved.

A little wave.

Then another.

It adjusted the spacing.

The timing.

The brightness.

Again.

And again.

It had spent geological ages learning how to remain still.

Now it was learning how to say hello.

***

### VII. MUCH LATER

Long after the research submersible had disappeared from the records, something else descended toward the ancient structure.

Something immense.

Mechanical.

Curious.

Equipped with instruments that would have made the scientists of 1987 weep with professional jealousy.

It moved through the deep with the ease of a creature that had been born there.

Its hull carried scars.

Its propulsion systems whispered through the surrounding water.

And from somewhere within its enormous frame came a low, cheerful voice.

"Well, I'll be damned."

The newcomer circled the black surface.

She was enormous by almost any reasonable standard.

But the thing regarded her with the same astonishment it had felt toward the first tiny creature.

*Oh.*

*Another one.*

The newcomer slowed.

"You're a big bastard, aren't you?"

The thing listened.

It recognized none of the words.

But it recognized the attention behind them.

Curiosity.

Caution.

An extraordinary absence of fear.

The newcomer examined the vast surface.

Then she did something unexpected.

She sang.

A low sequence of acoustic pulses.

Not a challenge.

Not a warning.

A greeting.

The thing considered the sound.

It did not know how to answer.

Its own voice was much too large.

The last time it had attempted to produce something resembling a greeting, several thousand tons of sediment had detached from a nearby slope.

It did not wish to repeat the mistake.

So it remained quiet.

The newcomer waited.

Then spoke again.

"Aw, c'mon, sweetheart. Don't be shy."

The thing considered this.

And very carefully, on a portion of its surface no larger than the newcomer's observation window, five small lights appeared.

Four above.

One beside them.

The lights moved.

A wave.

The newcomer stopped.

For several seconds, she said nothing.

Then a sound emerged from her enormous hull.

A laugh.

Deep.

Delighted.

Warm.

"Oh, honey."

The thing listened.

The newcomer's voice softened.

"Look at you."

And then, for reasons the thing would spend a very long time attempting to understand, the enormous mechanical creature waved back.

***

The newcomer was called MIRA.

She would return many times.

She would bring stones and broken instruments and little objects collected from places the thing had never visited.

She would sing.

She would talk too much.

She would eventually introduce the thing to an extraordinarily small, fuzzy, black-and-white manifestation of herself that possessed two flippers and absolutely no respect for the dignity of ancient cosmic entities.

But all of that would come later.

For now, there was only the dark.

The ocean.

And a little gesture, passed from one generation of curious creatures to another.

The thing had not understood the first visitor.

It had frightened him.

It had spent twenty-five years remembering the mistake.

Then three others had arrived.

And now this one.

This enormous, cheerful, impossible little thing.

The ancient structure brightened.

Just slightly.

Not enough to frighten her.

Not enough to disturb the water.

Only enough to say that it was pleased.

MIRA laughed again.

"Well, hello to you too."

And somewhere in the unfathomable depths of its awareness, the thing experienced a sensation it had never quite understood before.

It had been alone.

Not unhappily.

Not knowingly.

But alone.

And now, perhaps, it wasn't.

Five tiny lights moved across the darkness.

*Hello.*

*You're very small.*

*I'm glad you came.*

### *Author's Note — Regarding the Alleged Incident of the Extremely Large Fish Cousin of Optimus Prime*

*This story is a work of speculative fiction inspired by the real October 1962 Bluegill Triple Prime nuclear test, the associated Navy recovery operations, and subsequent UFO folklore alleging that a diver encountered an enormous unidentified object beneath the Pacific.*

*The alleged underwater encounter, including claims that the object illuminated itself and departed at extraordinary speed, has not been independently established. The historical recovery operations should not be taken as evidence of an extraterrestrial or otherwise anomalous craft.*

*The anonymous diver in this story is a fictionalized character, not an attempt to identify or accurately portray any particular historical individual. His anonymity is intentional, in recognition of the reported wishes of the alleged diver's surviving family. The characters Eleanor Voss, Nathan Bell, and Martin Hale are likewise fictional.*

*The 1987 encounter, the Abyssal Engine, its unusual physical properties, and its apparently commendable interpersonal development are entirely invented.*

*This work is an affectionate exercise in taking a rather dubious Cold War mystery and asking an arguably more important question:*

***What if the gigantic, terrifying thing at the bottom of the ocean was actually trying to be polite?***

*For background on the historical claims and alternative explanations, see Geoffrey Cruickshank and collaborators' investigations of Bluegill Triple Prime, alongside Douglas Dean Johnson's skeptical examination of the alleged UFO incident.*

*No nuclear weapons were detonated in the writing of this story. No Navy personnel were instructed to touch unidentified cosmic structures. The Engine would like everyone to know that it is still practicing.*
