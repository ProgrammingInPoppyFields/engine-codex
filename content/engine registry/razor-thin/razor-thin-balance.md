# MUSEUM HOURS

![](/images/470.png)

BALANCING ON THE RAZOR-THIN ENGINE

No one applauds
when you make it across
another ordinary minute.

The crossing is too small
for ceremony.

One foot placed
on a silver line
so narrow
it has stopped pretending
to be a road.

Below you,
the dark does not threaten.

It waits.

This is almost worse.

The RAZOR-THIN ENGINE
does not roar.
It does not shake the sky.
It does not announce
that you are in danger.

It glows.

Beautifully.

Like the universe
has mistaken your fear
for an aesthetic decision.

A ribbon of silver aurora
winds over the chasm,
thin as a corrected sentence,
thin as the breath
you do not let become sound.

You step.

The light bends.

Not enough to break.

Never enough
to give you proof.

That is the cruelty.

If it broke,
someone might understand.

If you fell,
the shape of the fall
would become evidence.

But you do not fall.

You stand in the kitchen
holding a trash bag
like it is an artifact
from a civilization
that believed errands
were simple.

You check the lock.

You check your phone.

You do not check the news.

You check your phone again.

You do not search
the thing
that would give the fear
a thousand new doors.

You do not let the thought
become architecture.

You do not let suspicion
furnish the room.

You do not let the room
become a trial.

This takes work.

Not inspirational work.

Not cinematic work.

No training montage.
No violin.
No clean white caption
about resilience.

This is animal work.

Nervous system work.

Jaw unclenched by force.
Hands washed twice, not six times.
Window noticed, then ignored.
Breath counted
until the body agrees
to remain a body
and not a prophecy.

This is the labor
of not spiraling
while standing beside
a mailbox.

This is the labor
of walking past
a parked car
without assigning it
a motive.

This is the labor
of refusing
to turn dread
into evidence
just because dread
brought paperwork.

The Engine hums
under your feet.

Not kind.
Not cruel.

Only exact.

It teaches the body
a terrible mathematics:

how much attention
must be spent
to perform one normal gesture,

how much terror
can be folded
into taking out the trash,

how long a person
can survive
inside the word functional,

how steady
a wound can look
when it has learned
not to spill.

You answer the message.
You hold the cup.
You fetch the mail.
You make the meeting.
You keep your voice level.
You laugh
at the correct place.

Everyone sees balance.

No one sees
the thousand invisible corrections
required
to remain upright.

Do not click that.
Do not rehearse that.
Do not map exits again.
Do not build the enemy
out of shadows.
Do not mistake adrenaline
for revelation.
Do not call panic
a god
just because it speaks
with certainty.

You keep walking.

Not because you are brave.

Bravery has banners.
Bravery gets remembered.

This is smaller.

This is the hand
on the doorknob.

This is the foot
over the threshold.

This is the trash
carried down the hall
while the mind offers
every possible catastrophe
with professional sincerity.

This is the mailbox
opened
like a mouth
that might accuse you.

This is the ordinary world
requiring proof
that you can enter it
without making a religion
out of fear.

This is the sentence:

I’m fine.

Translated correctly:

I have spent everything
to remain here.

Ahead,
the silver ribbon narrows.

Of course it does.

The RAZOR-THIN ENGINE
is not interested
in arrival.

It is interested
in continuation.

So you cross
another impossible inch,

held up
by a light
that was never wide enough
to be called mercy

and never false enough
to disappear.

And somewhere,
because of this labor
no one saw,

the trash reaches the curb.

The mail comes inside.

The door closes.

The body remains.

The day,
unimpressed,
continues.
