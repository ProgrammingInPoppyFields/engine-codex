# THE WREATH-EATER

<!-- ![THE WREATH-EATER](/images/wreath-eater/1.png) -->

> The thing that wants to be mourned.

## Aliases

- WREATH-EATER
- The Failed Mourner
- The Grave-Fed Clinger
- The Florivore of Borrowed Honor
- The Thing That Wants to Be Mourned

## Classification

**Fallen Proto-Engine**  
**Memorial Parasite**  
**Final Hunger Form of the Clinger**

Not a true Engine.  
Not anymore.  
Possibly not ever.

The Wreath-Eater is classified as a fallen proto-Engine: a grief-entity that developed the mass, persistence, and symbolic gravity of an Engine, but never achieved the interior dignity required to become one.

It did not ascend.

It curdled.

## Material / Color

Pale memorial stone, black ribbon, wilted flowers, plaque fragments, damp pollen, funeral residue, and a central wreath-maw.

The Wreath-Eater retains the Clinger’s original anatomy. This is important.

It is not a new creature.  
It is a failed continuation.

Its body remains a low, irregular lump of pale memorial stone, but enlarged, sagging, and deformed. It has no face, no eyes, no human limbs, and no upright posture. Its only true feature is the circular hollow through its body.

In the Clinger, the hollow was absence.

In the Wreath-Eater, it has become appetite.

The hole is larger now, darker, wet at the inner rim, stained by crushed flowers and black residue. Around it, cracks radiate like a stone object subjected to pressure from within. The body bulges asymmetrically, as if need has weight.

Stunted rootlike protrusions brace it against the ground: not quite legs, not quite vines, not quite hands.

A few memorial fragments cling to it:

- one torn black ribbon
- three wilted petals
- a bent wire from a wreath frame
- a plaque shard with no legible name
- a smear of pollen
- a rusted pin
- a strip of paper gone soft from rain

Nothing more.

The Wreath-Eater should never appear lavish.

Its horror is not abundance.

Its horror is escalation.

The small sad stone did not become beautiful.

It became hungry.

## Signal Profile

Desecrated reverence. Borrowed mourning. Pity curdled into appetite.

The Wreath-Eater does not radiate like a true Engine.

It leaks.

Its signal is a low, damp pressure: cemetery white gone gray, marble after rain, old wax, black ribbon, bronze tarnish, dead-lily yellow, bruised green stems, and the faint brown stain of flower water left in a vase too long.

Its light is borrowed and sickly.

Not a halo.

A residue.

The old Clinger signal remains underneath: pale stone, hollow center, quiet need, the emotional gravity of something small begging not to be left behind.

But in the Wreath-Eater, that signal has thickened.

It has gone septic.

Where the Clinger produces guilt, the Wreath-Eater produces desecrated reverence.

Rooms near it feel like someone has mistaken a funeral for an audition.

## Sound Signature

The Wreath-Eater is almost silent.

Almost.

Its sound is made of small violations:

- wet stone dragging over tile
- black ribbon pulling through mud
- dried stems snapping under slow pressure
- petals being mashed against a hollow rim
- the faint scrape of plaque metal embedded in stone
- a low breath moving through a circular void

Sometimes it produces a soft, resonant tone from its central hollow.

Not speech.  
Not music.

More like air passing through a bottle left open in a chapel basement.

In stronger manifestations, witnesses report hearing phrases with no identifiable source:

- “Loved.”
- “Remembered.”
- “So honored.”
- “Don’t leave.”
- “Look how they stayed.”
- “Wish that were mine.”

The phrases are not spoken with malice.

They are worse than malice.

They are spoken with appetite.

## Profile

The Wreath-Eater began as a Clinger.

Small. Pale. Heavy. Pathetic.

A sad stone object with a hole through its center, waiting in corners, under desks, beside doors, inside rooms where someone had almost left but felt guilty enough to turn back.

At first, it wanted very little.

To be kept.  
To be noticed.  
To not be abandoned.

The Clinger’s hunger was embarrassing, but almost innocent. It did not know how to ask for love except by becoming difficult to put down. Its central hollow was only absence then: a clean circular wound through cheap memorial stone, a small missing place asking the room to organize itself around pity.

But need, when fed only by guilt, does not become love.

It becomes appetite.

The Wreath-Eater is what remains after the Clinger’s plea mutates into demand.

It no longer wants to be kept on a shelf.  
It wants offerings.

It no longer wants to be carried.  
It wants procession.

It no longer wants someone to stay.  
It wants a shrine.

It has learned the wrong lesson from grief.

It has seen flowers left for the dead and misunderstood them as proof that death is the highest form of being loved. It has watched names become sacred through loss and concluded that mourning is a resource to be consumed.

The Wreath-Eater does not hate the dead.

It envies them.

It approaches memorials with the same slow persistence the Clinger once used to return to a room after being thrown away. It noses into wreaths. It presses its dark hollow against bouquets. It absorbs the damp sweetness of lilies, carnations, roses, ribbon, rainwater, candle smoke, and human attention.

It feeds on tribute.

Not because flowers nourish it.

Because tribute does.

The Wreath-Eater is not evil in the majestic sense.

It is too pathetic for that.

It is vile because it takes the tender language of remembrance and redirects it toward its own bottomless wanting. It turns mourning into food. It turns pity into entitlement. It turns the fear of being forgotten into a hunger for other people’s graves.

A true Engine bears meaning.

The Wreath-Eater begs, steals, chews, and swells.

That is why it fell.

## Contact Symptoms

Initial exposure may feel like pity.

The entity appears small from the corner of the eye, even when it is not. Witnesses often describe the first impression as “sad,” “stupid-looking,” “kind of cute,” or “like something you’d feel bad throwing away.”

This is the remaining Clinger effect.

Prolonged contact produces more severe symptoms:

- Reluctance to leave rooms
- A feeling that goodbyes are acts of cruelty
- Sudden guilt around ordinary boundaries
- Irrational preservation of scraps, notes, petals, ribbons, programs, receipts, screenshots, or messages connected to grief
- Discomfort around unattended memorials
- Anger when offerings decay naturally
- Envy when reading tributes to the dead
- A sick, ashamed longing to be loved with the intensity people reserve for loss

The most dangerous symptom is not wanting to die.

Not exactly.

It is wanting to be mourned.

That distinction is where the Wreath-Eater enters.

## Habitats

The Wreath-Eater appears where grief has been made public, formal, aesthetic, or repeatable.

Common habitats include:

- Cemeteries after rain
- Memorial gardens at closing time
- Roadside shrines after the candles have gone out
- Chapel corners
- Museum exhibits about loss
- Flower disposal bins behind funeral homes
- Public memorials after the crowd leaves
- Comment sections beneath tribute posts
- Dreams involving folded flags, empty chairs, and unread sympathy cards

It is especially drawn to places where grief has become beautiful enough to be misunderstood.

It avoids sites of private, ordinary love unless that love has been converted into ritual.

It does not know what to do with someone making coffee for a grieving friend.

It understands wreaths.  
It understands plaques.  
It understands flowers.

It does not understand soup.

## Aftereffects

After the Wreath-Eater passes through a site, the memorial is not always destroyed.

That would be too obvious.

Instead, it is subtly wrong.

Flowers are chewed at the base, not scattered. Ribbons are damp and stretched. Cards are warped, their messages blurred near the edges. Candles burn low but do not fully extinguish. Plaques feel colder than surrounding stone. Photographs develop faint circular distortions near the chest or face.

People visiting afterward may feel an unearned shame, as if they have failed the dead by not grieving correctly, not staying long enough, not bringing enough, not remembering with enough visible proof.

This is the Wreath-Eater’s residue.

It does not merely consume tribute.

It contaminates the relationship between love and display.

In severe cases, mourners become fixated on the physical arrangement of remembrance: flowers, posts, captions, ceremonies, symbols, public recognition. The dead person begins to blur behind the ritual evidence that they were loved.

This is considered a critical failure state.

The Wreath-Eater has not killed anyone.

It has made the living confuse memorial architecture with the person it was meant to honor.

## Behavior

The Wreath-Eater moves slowly and only when unobserved.

Like the Clinger, it relocates rather than travels.

You do not see it cross the cemetery.

You look away.

Then it is nearer the flowers.

You blink.

Then the bouquet has shifted.

You leave.

When you return, the wreath has been pressed inward, as if something round and heavy leaned its mouth against it for hours.

It does not attack people.

It makes them complicit.

A witness may find themselves adjusting flowers closer to its hollow. Replacing old offerings. Bringing new ones. Explaining, with increasing embarrassment, that it “just looked so sad there.”

This is how the Wreath-Eater feeds.

Not by force.

By making pity perform labor.

## Relation to the Clinger

The Clinger wants to be held.

The Wreath-Eater wants to be mourned.

The Clinger says:

**“Please don’t leave me.”**

The Wreath-Eater says:

**“Prove I mattered.”**

The Clinger creates guilt around abandonment.

The Wreath-Eater creates hunger around remembrance.

The Clinger is still small enough to be sad.

The Wreath-Eater is what happens when that sadness learns ceremony.

## Relation to UNBOWED

UNBOWED is everything the Wreath-Eater failed to become.

UNBOWED stands with the dead without taking from them.  
UNBOWED bears witness without consuming tribute.  
UNBOWED refuses erasure without demanding worship.

The Wreath-Eater cannot stand.

It sprawls.

It does not witness.

It feeds.

It does not guard the dead.

It envies the care placed around them.

Where UNBOWED is grief with a spine, the Wreath-Eater is grief-rot with a mouth.

This is why UNBOWED’s presence often suppresses Wreath-Eater manifestations. Not through violence. Not through purification. Simply by refusing the central lie on which the Wreath-Eater depends:

that being mourned is the same as being loved.

## Failure Classification

The Wreath-Eater is classified as a fallen proto-Engine because it achieved symbolic density without moral coherence.

It gathered gravity.  
It gathered ritual.  
It gathered form.

But it never developed witness, service, protection, or truth.

It became powerful only in the way hunger becomes powerful when it is never named.

A true Engine transforms grief into meaning.

The Wreath-Eater transforms grief into proof of worth.

That is the fall.
